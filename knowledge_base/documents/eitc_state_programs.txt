State EITC Programs - Supplemental Credits by State

Overview of State Earned Income Tax Credits

Many states and localities offer their own EITC that supplements the federal credit. These state credits are usually calculated as a percentage of your federal EITC.

States with EITC Programs (2024)

REFUNDABLE STATE EITCs (you can get money back even if you owe no state tax):

California (CalEITC):
- Percentage: Separate calculation, not based on federal EITC
- Must file state return to claim
- California Young Child Tax Credit also available
- Additional foster youth credit available
- CalEIT

C Assistant available online

Colorado:
- Percentage: 25% of federal EITC (increasing in future years)
- Refundable
- Must file state return

Connecticut:
- Percentage: 30.5% of federal EITC (higher for certain filers)
- Refundable
- No state income requirement

Delaware:
- Percentage: 4.5% of federal EITC
- Refundable for certain income levels
- Non-refundable for higher incomes

District of Columbia:
- Percentage: 70% of federal EITC
- Fully refundable
- One of the most generous state credits

Illinois:
- Percentage: 20% of federal EITC
- Fully refundable
- Must file state return

Indiana:
- Percentage: 9% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Must have qualifying children

Iowa:
- Percentage: 15% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Adjusted annually for inflation

Kansas:
- Percentage: 17% of federal EITC
- Refundable for certain filers
- Different rules based on income

Louisiana:
- Percentage: 5% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Must file state return

Maine:
- Percentage: 15% of federal EITC (without qualifying children)
- Percentage: 25% of federal EITC (with qualifying children)
- Fully refundable

Maryland:
- Percentage: 50% of federal EITC (for most filers)
- Percentage: 100% of federal EITC (for certain low-income filers)
- Non-custodial parent credit also available
- Refundable

Massachusetts:
- Percentage: 40% of federal EITC
- Fully refundable
- Available even if no federal income tax liability

Michigan:
- Percentage: 6% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Must have qualifying children

Minnesota:
- Separate calculation, not percentage-based
- Working family credit
- Fully refundable
- More generous than many states

Montana:
- Percentage: 3% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Must file state return

Nebraska:
- Percentage: 10% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Available for all qualifying households

New Jersey:
- Percentage: 40% of federal EITC
- Fully refundable
- Additional credits for certain workers

New Mexico:
- Percentage: 17% of federal EITC
- Fully refundable
- Must file state return

New York:
- Percentage: 30% of federal EITC
- NYC residents get additional 5%
- Fully refundable
- Noncustodial parent EITC also available

Ohio:
- Percentage: 30% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Must have qualifying children

Oklahoma:
- Percentage: 5% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Must file state return

Oregon:
- Percentage: 9% of federal EITC
- Fully refundable
- Earned Income Credit available

Rhode Island:
- Percentage: 15% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Must file state return

South Carolina:
- Percentage: 125% of federal EITC liability reduction
- Refundable portion available
- Complex calculation

Vermont:
- Percentage: 36% of federal EITC
- Fully refundable
- Must file state return

Virginia:
- Percentage: 15% of federal EITC
- Refundable
- Must file state return and qualify federally

Washington:
- Working Families Tax Credit
- Up to $1,200 per year
- Separate application required
- Not directly tied to federal EITC percentage

Wisconsin:
- Percentage: Varies by family size and filing status
- 4% (no children) to 11% (three+ children)
- Fully refundable

NON-REFUNDABLE STATE EITCs (can only reduce state tax owed, no refund):

States with non-refundable credits generally allow you to reduce your state tax liability, but you won't receive a refund if the credit exceeds your tax owed.

Local/County EITCs:

Some localities have their own EITC programs:
- Montgomery County, Maryland
- New York City (in addition to state)
- San Francisco, California (in addition to state)
- Check your local government for programs

How to Claim State EITC

STEP 1: File Your Federal Return First
- Calculate your federal EITC
- Keep a copy of your federal return

STEP 2: Check Your State's Requirements
- Some states require a separate application
- Others automatically calculate it on state return
- Washington requires separate application

STEP 3: File State Tax Return
- Even if you don't owe state tax, file to claim EITC
- Reference your federal EITC amount
- Provide any additional documentation required

STEP 4: Keep Records
- State audits can happen independently of federal
- Keep same documentation as federal EITC

Special State Provisions

California CalEITC:
- Has its own income limits (lower than federal)
- Different calculation methodology
- Additional credits available:
  - Young Child Tax Credit (children under 6)
  - Foster Youth Tax Credit
- Must have earned income of at least $1

Minnesota Working Family Credit:
- Uses its own formula
- Not a simple percentage of federal EITC
- Generally more generous than percentage-based credits
- Separate income limits

Washington Working Families Tax Credit:
- Unique application process
- Not connected to state tax return (Washington has no income tax)
- Must apply separately through Department of Revenue
- Claim online at WorkingFamiliesCredit.wa.gov

State EITC Income Limits

Most state EITCs use the same income limits as federal EITC:
- If you qualify for federal EITC, you likely qualify for state EITC
- Some states (like California) have separate, lower limits
- Check your specific state's requirements

State vs. Federal EITC Example

Example: Single parent with 2 children in Maryland

Federal EITC: $5,000 (example amount)
Maryland EITC: $5,000 × 50% = $2,500

Total EITC benefit: $7,500

The state credit provides additional money on top of the federal credit!

Benefits of State EITC

1. Additional money to qualifying families
2. Helps offset state and local taxes
3. Boosts economic impact of federal EITC
4. Some states have more generous age/income rules
5. Puts more money into local economies

Common State EITC Mistakes

1. Not filing state return because you don't owe tax
   - File anyway to get refundable credit!

2. Forgetting to claim state EITC on state return
   - Many returns have a line for it - don't skip it

3. Not meeting state-specific requirements
   - Some states have residency requirements
   - Some have different income calculations

4. Missing separate applications
   - Washington requires separate application
   - Some states have additional forms

5. Not checking for local credits
   - Your city or county may have additional credits
   - These are often overlooked

Changes to Watch For

State EITC programs change:
- Percentages may increase or decrease
- New states may add programs
- Refundability may change
- Always check current year rules

Why States Offer EITC

Policy Goals:
- Reduce poverty
- Support working families
- Offset regressive state taxes
- Economic stimulus
- Child wellbeing
- Work incentive

Economic Impact:
- Every dollar of EITC generates local economic activity
- Families typically spend credits on necessities
- Benefits local businesses
- Reduces reliance on other assistance programs

Future of State EITC

Trends:
- More states considering EITC programs
- Existing programs often expanding
- Increased percentages in many states
- More attention to childless workers
- Potential federal incentives for state programs

Final State EITC Tips

1. Always file your state return, even if you don't owe tax
2. Claim your state EITC even if small
3. Check for local/county credits too
4. Some states allow prior-year claims
5. State credits are additional money - not a replacement
6. If you move mid-year, check which state's credit you can claim
7. Part-year residents may have prorated credits
8. Keep state and federal documentation together
9. State refunds typically arrive separately from federal
10. Some states have faster refund processing than IRS

Remember: State EITC is FREE MONEY you've earned. Don't leave it unclaimed!

Source: Various state revenue department websites, National Conference of State Legislatures, Tax Policy Center, Center on Budget and Policy Priorities
